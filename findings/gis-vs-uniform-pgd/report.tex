\section{Introduction}

Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (SPD) \cite{bushnaq2025stochasticparameterdecomposition}
decomposes neural network parameters into sparsely used subcomponents via the objective
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPD}}
    = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{faithfulness}}
    + \sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i \mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{recon}}
    + \beta_{n+1} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{importance\text{-}minimality}}.
\end{align*}
Its reconstruction losses are a major computational cost because they evaluate
reconstruction error under different subcomponent masks. VPD revises this objective in
several ways \cite{bushnaq2026interpreting}; the one relevant here is that alongside a
stochastic reconstruction loss it adds an adversarial one, in which gradient ascent finds
a high-loss mask before the model is optimized against it.

The stochastic reconstruction terms currently sample masking sources uniformly. We
propose \textbf{Gradient-Informed Sampling (GIS)}, which biases a stochastic draw toward
subcomponents with high reconstruction-loss sensitivity. The purpose of this report is
to ask the VPD authors one question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Would replacing VPD's uniform stochastic mask sources with GIS sources be useful
while leaving its adversarial (PGD-optimized) reconstruction term unchanged? If so,
which model and configuration would provide the most informative first experiment?}
\end{quote}

The evidence we offer is narrow: GIS finds higher-loss masks than uniform sampling at
low marginal cost, and improves standalone training on \texttt{resid\_mlp2}. PGD is
included as an optimization-based reference, not as a competing sampler.

\section{Background}

Formally speaking, the reconstruction part in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SPD}}$ aims to solve
\begin{align} \label{eq:abl_req}
    \min_{U,V, \theta} \ \mathbb{E}_{r\sim p} \left[ D\left(f\left(x \mid W^{\prime}\left(x, r \right)\right), f(x \mid W)\right) \right],
\end{align}
where $U$ and $V$ contain subcomponents, $\theta$ contain the MLP parameters for the causal importance functions $g$, and $p$ is some probability distribution with full support on $[0,1]^{C \times L}$.\footnote{$C, L, D, f, x, W$ follow the same notation as in the SPD paper.}
The expectation is intractable, so $\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i \mathcal{L}^{(i)}_{\text{recon}}$ in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SPD}}$ estimates it from a few sampled sources. Sampling the sources $r$ that dominate the expectation makes each such estimate more informative for minimizing \eqref{eq:abl_req}.\footnote{This is directly targeted by the adversarial reconstruction loss via an inner optimization.}


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

GIS reuses the sensitivity from one reconstruction term,
$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}$, to sample the next. For subcomponent $c$ in layer $l$,
it assigns weight\footnote{Implemented in
\href{https://github.com/chandanms/spd/blob/feature/important-sampling/spd/utils/component_utils.py}{\texttt{spd/utils/component\_utils.py}}.}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:weights}
    w_c^l(x) =
    \frac{\left|\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}/\partial g_c^l(x)\right|^k}
    {\sum_{c'}\left|\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}/
    \partial g_{c'}^l(x)\right|^k}.
\end{align}
It then draws $r_c^l\sim U[0,1]$ and substitutes $\widetilde r_c^l$ for $r_c^l$ in SPD's
ablation curve,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:mask}
    \widetilde r_c^l=\left(1-w_c^l(x)\right)r_c^l,
    \qquad
    m_c^l=g_c^l+(1-g_c^l)\widetilde r_c^l.
\end{align}
Large gradient magnitude therefore causes stronger ablation, while $k$ redistributes a
fixed budget, since $\sum_c w_c^l(x)=1$. Because $\widetilde r_c^l\le 1-w_c^l(x)$, the
GIS draw never samples sources above this cap, so it does not cover all of
$[0,1]^{C\times L}$ as assumed for $p$ in \eqref{eq:abl_req}. At $k=1$,
$w_c^l(x)\approx 1/C$ and the excluded range is negligible; it grows with $k$ (see the
coverage concern in Section~\ref{sec:vpd}). The gradients are detached before constructing the GIS source,
so the outer update does not differentiate through the sampling rule.

GIS reuses the forward pass of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}$ and adds one partial
backward pass. PGD cannot reuse it: each ascent step changes the mask, so each step
needs a new forward and backward pass.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gradient-Informed Sampling}
\label{alg:gis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require subcomponents $U,V$, causal importance parameters $\theta$, training data
$\mathcal X$, exponent $k$
\For{each outer training step}
    \State Sample $x$ and compute $y^*=f_{\mathrm{target}}(x)$
    \State Compute $\{g_c^l(\theta)\}=\Gamma_\theta(x)$
    \State Draw $u_c^l\sim U[0,1]$ and set $m_c^{(1),l}=g_c^l+(1-g_c^l)u_c^l$
    \State $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}=D\big(f(x\mid W'(x,\{m_c^{(1),l}\})),\,y^*\big)$
    \State $s_c^l=\operatorname{sg}\big[\partial\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}/\partial g_c^l\big]$
    \Comment{stop-gradient}
    \State Obtain $w_c^l(x)$ from $s_c^l$ using \eqref{eq:weights}
    \State Draw $r_c^l\sim U[0,1]$ and construct $\widetilde r_c^l,m_c^l$ using \eqref{eq:mask}
    \State $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(2)}=D\big(f(x\mid W'(x,\{m_c^l\})),\,y^*\big)$
    \State $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{total}}=\beta_1\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(1)}
    +\beta_2\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}^{(2)}
    +\beta_3\mathcal L_{\mathrm{importance\text{-}minimality}}$
    \State $(U,V,\theta)\gets\operatorname{OptimizerStep}
    (U,V,\theta;\nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{total}})$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Direct sampler benchmark}
\label{sec:sampler_benchmark}

We first isolate the sampling question from training. A \texttt{resid\_mlp2}
ComponentModel\footnote{\href{https://github.com/chandanms/spd/blob/feature/important-sampling/spd/models/component_model.py}{\texttt{spd/models/component\_model.py}}.}
is trained with uniform sampling and frozen at the final checkpoint.
Uniform, GIS, and PGD then see the same evaluation batches and use the same mask
construction. We report the reconstruction error induced by each mask,
$\Delta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{recon}}$; larger values indicate harder masks.

PGD starts from a uniform source and performs sign-gradient ascent on this same
objective with step size $0.5$. Its results calibrate the scale of the GIS improvement;
they are not a like-for-like sampler comparison. The weight-delta mask remains random
in every arm so that only the subcomponent source changes.

GIS consistently finds harder masks than uniform sampling, and the advantage increases
from $1.14\times$ at $k=1$ to $1.37\times$ at $k=8$. The result establishes the intended
mechanism: gradient magnitude contains useful information for a subsequent stochastic
draw.

The same table also bounds the importance of this result. Twenty-step PGD finds
$13.89\times$ the uniform reconstruction loss, while GIS recovers only 2.9\% of the
gap between uniform and PGD-20. Even one PGD step is much stronger than GIS, at the cost
of an additional forward pass (Section~\ref{sec:method}). GIS should therefore be evaluated against uniform, not
presented as an alternative to adversarial optimization.

\section{Training results}

Figure~\ref{fig:training-metrics} compares two uniform baselines, GIS, and PGD on
\texttt{resid\_mlp2}. GIS substantially reduces target-solution error and adversarial
reconstruction loss relative to both uniform baselines. PGD achieves the lowest
\texttt{PGDReconLoss}, but also the highest importance-minimality loss; GIS lies between
uniform sampling and PGD on both measures.

In the hyperparameter sweep, the best GIS run reaches an evaluation PGD reconstruction
loss of $1.86\times10^{-4}$ and target-solution error of $18.6$, versus
$1.57\times10^{-4}$ and $12.0$ for PGD.

Figure~\ref{fig:wallclock} shows the measured cost of each method. PGD training time
grows with the number of inner steps, from $1996$ seconds for the base PGD run to
$2976$ seconds at five steps. GIS stays between $1918$ and $2149$ seconds across its loss coefficients, so
five-step PGD takes $1.4$--$1.6\times$ as long as GIS.

Thus, on \texttt{resid\_mlp2}, GIS provides a substantially better standalone training
signal than uniform sampling and approaches PGD on several decomposition metrics. It
does not match PGD's adversarial reconstruction performance. All GIS training runs use
$k=1$, the weakest setting in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, so these results also do not
establish whether the harder draws produced by larger $k$ improve training.

\section{Proposed use in VPD}
\label{sec:vpd}

VPD uses separate adversarial and uniformly sampled reconstruction losses. On
\texttt{resid\_mlp2}, GIS improves standalone training over uniform sampling and
approaches PGD on several metrics. These results do not establish whether GIS adds value
when PGD is already present: the two methods may emphasize overlapping weaknesses, or
GIS may improve the stochastic term at low additional cost. This is the question posed
in the introduction: whether to replace VPD's uniform stochastic mask sources with GIS
sources while leaving its adversarial (PGD-optimized) reconstruction term unchanged.

The corresponding ablation would be
\begin{equation*}
 \text{PGD + uniform} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \text{PGD + GIS},
\end{equation*}
at matched PGD steps, loss coefficients, and seeds, with wall-clock time and memory
reported separately. Evaluation should report final decomposition metrics and
adversarial reconstruction loss.

Three uncertainties remain. First, VPD retains stochastic reconstruction to support
average-case reconstruction and avoid over-focusing on worst-case ablations; biasing
these draws may reduce that coverage. Second, although GIS improves standalone training
on \texttt{resid\_mlp2}, this does not establish that it improves training when combined
with PGD or at VPD's language-model scale. Third, the cost of the partial backward pass
and its activation memory has not been measured at that scale.

\section{Conclusion}

In the frozen-model benchmark, GIS induces up to 37\% higher reconstruction loss than
uniform sampling, showing that it finds harder masks. It also substantially improves
standalone training on \texttt{resid\_mlp2}, approaching PGD on several metrics at lower
cost. It does not match PGD's adversarial reconstruction performance, nor do our
experiments show that GIS adds value when PGD is already present. The supported next
step is to compare GIS against uniform sampling within VPD's stochastic reconstruction
term while leaving its adversarial (PGD-optimized) reconstruction term unchanged.
